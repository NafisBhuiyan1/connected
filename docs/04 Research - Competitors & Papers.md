## 3. Papers (citations verified to exist)

**Team formation and optimization**
1. **Lappas, T., Liu, K., & Terzi, E. (2009).** Finding a team of experts in social networks. *KDD '09*, 467-476. doi:10.1145/1557019.1557074. https://dblp.org/rec/conf/kdd/LappasLT09
   - Takeaway: the problem is to cover the required skills while keeping communication cost low across the network. It's NP-hard, so Connected should promise good approximate teams, not optimal ones.
2. **Anagnostopoulos, A., Becchetti, L., Castillo, C., Gionis, A., & Leonardi, S. (2012).** Online team formation in social networks. *WWW '12*, 839-848. doi:10.1145/2187836.2187950. https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Online-Team-Formation-in-Social-Networks.pdf
   - Takeaway: tasks arrive one after another, and you cover skills while **balancing workload**. That maps to many project requests competing for the same few strong people.
3. **Nemhauser, G. L., Wolsey, L. A., & Fisher, M. L. (1978).** An analysis of approximations for maximizing submodular set functions—I. *Mathematical Programming*, 14(1), 265-294. doi:10.1007/BF01588971. https://dblp.org/rec/journals/mp/NemhauserWF78.html
   - Takeaway: for a monotone submodular objective with a size limit, greedy is guaranteed at least **(1−1/e) ≈ 63%** of optimal.
   - This only holds if Connected's objective really is monotone submodular (weighted skill coverage is). Pairwise-conflict penalties can break the guarantee.
4. **Bhowmik, A., Borkar, V., Garg, D., & Pallan, M. (2014).** Submodularity in team formation problem. *SDM 2014*, 893-901. https://dblp.org/rec/conf/sdm/BhowmikBGP14
   - Takeaway: team formation written as a submodular problem, which is precedent for the greedy approach.
5. **Layton, R. A., Loughry, M. L., Ohland, M. W., & Ricco, G. D. (2010).** Design and validation of a web-based system for assigning members to teams using instructor-specified criteria. *Advances in Engineering Education*, 2(1). https://advances.asee.org/wp-content/uploads/vol02/issue01/papers/aee-vol02-issue01-p09.pdf
   - Takeaway: the software met the stated team criteria more closely than experienced faculty. It's evidence that algorithmic formation works, and it's also prior art.

**Reciprocal recommendation and explanations**

6. **Pizzato, L., Rej, T., Chung, T., Koprinska, I., & Kay, J. (2010).** RECON: a reciprocal recommender for online dating. *RecSys '10*, 207-214. doi:10.1145/1864708.1864747. https://www.sciweavers.org/node/304331
   - Takeaway: scoring interest in both directions raised top-10 success from **26% to 45%**. Score whether a candidate would want to join, not only whether the requester wants them.
   - Survey of the field: Palomares, Porcel, Pizzato, Guy & Herrera-Viedma (2021), *Information Fusion* 69:103-127, doi:10.1016/j.inffus.2020.12.001. https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/reciprocal-recommender-systems-analysis-of-state-of-art-literatur-3/
7. **Herlocker, J. L., Konstan, J. A., & Riedl, J. (2000).** Explaining collaborative filtering recommendations. *CSCW '00*, 241-250. doi:10.1145/358916.358995. https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/cscw/HerlockerKR00.html
   - Takeaway: in experiments, explanations increased users' acceptance of recommendations. This supports "every match comes with a reason."

**Complementarity vs. homophily**

8. **McPherson, M., Smith-Lovin, L., & Cook, J. M. (2001).** Birds of a feather: Homophily in social networks. *Annual Review of Sociology*, 27, 415-444. doi:10.1146/annurev.soc.27.1.415. https://sil.asc.upenn.edu/files/McPherson-2001-ARS.pdf
   - Takeaway: similarity shapes ties of every kind, including work. Left alone, people pick people like themselves.
9. **Ruef, M., Aldrich, H. E., & Carter, N. M. (2003).** The structure of founding teams. *American Sociological Review*, 68(2), 195-222. doi:10.1177/000312240306800202. https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/742175
   - Takeaway: founding teams were shaped mostly by homophily and strong ties, not by functional need.
10. **Horwitz, S. K., & Horwitz, I. B. (2007).** The effects of team diversity on team outcomes. *Journal of Management*, 33(6), 987-1015. https://www.econbiz.de/Record/the-effects-of-team-diversity-on-team-outcomes-a-meta-analytic-review-of-team-demography-horwitz-sujin/10003586196
    - Takeaway (abstract level): diversity in task-related attributes was linked to performance. Demographic diversity was not.
11. **Guimerà, R., Uzzi, B., Spiro, J., & Amaral, L. A. N. (2005).** Team assembly mechanisms determine collaboration network structure and team performance. *Science*, 308(5722), 697-702. doi:10.1126/science.1106340. https://amarallab.github.io/media/publications/2005/Guimera-2005-Science-308-697.pdf
    - Takeaway: how a team gets assembled (newcomers vs. incumbents, repeat collaborators) affects how it performs.
12. **Nolte, A., Chounta, I.-A., & Herbsleb, J. D. (2020).** What happens to all these hackathon projects? *Proc. ACM HCI*, 4(CSCW2), Art. 145. doi:10.1145/3415216. https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10284449
    - Takeaway: in Devpost data, **long-term project continuation was predicted by team skill diversity.**
13. **Tisha, S. N., Sakib, M. N., & Dey, S. (2025).** Competing or collaborating? Hackathon formats and team dynamics. arXiv:2512.20181 (SIGCSE TS 2026). https://arxiv.org/html/2512.20181
    - Takeaway: at a regular campus hackathon, teams formed by familiarity and similar skills. 58 of 101 teams were all-male.

**Rejection and signaling** (indirect support for double opt-in)

14. **Boothby, E. J., Cooney, G., Sandstrom, G. M., & Clark, M. S. (2018).** The liking gap in conversations. *Psychological Science*, 29(11), 1742-1756. doi:10.1177/0956797618783714. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/you-probably-made-a-better-first-impression-than-you-think.html
    - Takeaway: people underestimate how much new partners like them.
15. **Sandstrom, G. M., & Boothby, E. J. (2021).** Why do people avoid talking to strangers? *Self and Identity*, 20(1), 47-71. doi:10.1080/15298868.2020.1816568. https://repository.essex.ac.uk/28669/
    - Takeaway: across 7 studies (N=2,304), fears about talking to strangers were overblown, and those fears predicted avoidance.
16. **Bapna, R., Ramaprasad, J., Shmueli, G., & Umyarov, A. (2016).** One-way mirrors in online dating: A randomized field experiment. *Management Science*, 62(11), 3100-3122. doi:10.1287/mnsc.2015.2301. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/references/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2301
    - Takeaway: when users lost the ability to send weak signals of interest, they got fewer matches. Low-cost mutual signals work.
17. **Casciaro, T., Gino, F., & Kouchaki, M. (2014).** How networking can make us feel dirty. *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 59(4), 705-735. doi:10.1177/0001839214554990. https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/the-contaminating-effects-of-building-instrumental-ties-how-networking-can-make-us-feel-dirty
    - Takeaway: professional networking makes people feel "dirty," so they do less of it.

**Gap in the literature:** I found **no study that directly tests whether double-opt-in intros reduce rejection anxiety.** Papers 14-16 are only indirect evidence.

## 4. Problem stats (sourced only)

**Event networking (all organizer or vendor surveys)**
- Freeman 2025, as reported by BizBash:
  - 51% of attendees say effective networking is reason enough to return to an event.
  - 58% were motivated by networking in 2024, up from 39% in 2021.
  - Among ages 23-46: **40% find networking awkward, 30% struggle to start conversations, and nearly 50% want curated connection recommendations before the event.**
  - https://www.bizbash.com/corporate-events/business-events-face-strategic-shift-as-networking-becomes-main-attendance-draw
- Bizzabo 2025 Event Networking Report (300+ respondents): https://www.bizzabo.com/blog/future-of-event-networking
  - 83% of attendees say networking shapes whether they attend.
  - **34% say follow-up after initial networking "often leads nowhere."**
  - 42% of organizers rate engagement with networking features as low.
  - 27% of organizers name poor matchmaking as a top challenge.
- Weak: an "UpVisit 2025" figure (78% attend B2B events to network, 56% dissatisfied) is cited only by a vendor blog, with no sample size given. https://www.converve.com/event-networking-blog/event-networking-vs-matchmaking

**Jobs and networking**
- MyPerfectResume (May 2025, 1,000 US workers): **54%** got hired through a personal or professional connection. https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2025/08/14/survey-54-of-workers-got-hired-through-a-connection/167378/

## 5. Differentiation gaps (what the evidence supports)

1. **Goal-first team formation for peers at an event.** A.Team does goal → roles → team, but for paid staffing. hackathon.camp, CATME, and gruepr are run by an organizer or instructor. None of the products I reviewed lets a *participant* describe a project, then has AI fill its roles from an opted-in pool at the same event. Say "in our review," not "first ever."
2. **A grounded reason per role.** Whova shows shared traits, and Grip's page doesn't mention explanations. "Why *this person* fills *this role* on *your* goal" goes further.
3. **Multi-party double opt-in.** YC, Boardy, and Teamless do double opt-in between two people. No reviewed product has every member of a team consent.
4. **Organizer demand-vs-supply view.** Grip Pulse already shows the attendee mix. What's new is skill *demand* inferred from project goals, compared against skill *supply*.
5. **Hackathon-native.** It directly replaces the CrownHacks/MLH-style mixer and Devpost's checkbox.
6. **Not differentiators:** AI-drafted icebreakers (LinkedIn "Write with AI," Whova Icebreaker), AI 1:1 recommendations, and double opt-in by itself.

## 6. Recommended pitch claims

### Safe (sourced)
- "CrownHacks requires 2-4 person teams and runs a mixer for people without one. Half of attendees are first-timers. We automate that mixer." (crownhacks.space)
- "Event platforms like Grip, Brella, Whova, Bizzabo, and Swapcard recommend people one-to-one. Hackathon platforms use a 'looking for teammates' checkbox. Connected forms whole teams around a goal."
- "Left alone, people form teams by similarity and familiarity (McPherson 2001, Ruef 2003, Tisha 2025). Skill diversity predicts whether hackathon projects keep going (Nolte 2020)."
- "Scoring both directions matters: reciprocity raised match success from 26% to 45% (Pizzato 2010)."
- "Explanations increase acceptance of recommendations (Herlocker 2000), so every match shows its evidence."
- "Team formation is NP-hard (Lappas 2009), so we use greedy optimization." Add "with a (1−1/e) guarantee (Nemhauser 1978)" **only if** the objective is monotone submodular.
- "People underestimate how much strangers like them (Boothby 2018). Double opt-in reveals mutual interest without one-sided rejection." Present this as design rationale, not a proven effect.
- "40% of younger professionals find networking awkward, and ~50% want curated recommendations before an event (Freeman 2025)."
- "54% of workers got hired through a connection (MyPerfectResume 2025)."

### Unsafe (don't say)
- "First/only AI that builds teams from a project idea." A.Team shipped this in 2024, and hackathon.camp, CATME, and gruepr form multi-person teams.
- "Optimal teams." The problem is NP-hard.
- "Double opt-in is proven to reduce rejection anxiety." No direct study was found.
- "85% of jobs come from networking." Weak source.
- "56% of attendees are dissatisfied with networking." Secondhand, no sample size.
- "Teams formed by Connected perform better." There's no outcome data yet.
- "AI icebreakers are new," or naming Lunchclub, Shapr, or Bumble Bizz as live competitors. All three are dormant, closed, or discontinued.
- Vendor uplift numbers (e.g. Swapcard "55%") presented as independent facts.
